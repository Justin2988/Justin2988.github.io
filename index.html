VA Electronic Health Record Modernization

Training, documentation, and Power BI dashboards supporting the VA's move of 170+ medical facilities from VistA to a new Federal electronic health record.
Role

Support Analyst & Consultant, VA OIT Support

Organization

Take2 Consulting, LLC

Dates

January 2022 – September 2026

Scale

170+ medical facilities

The situation

The VA is replacing VistA, the system that has run its hospitals for decades, with a single Federal electronic health record built on Oracle Health. Every facility that switches over has its own staff, workflows, and problems. The program produces a huge amount of status data, but it's spread across many systems, and the people making decisions need it pulled together in time to act.

What I did

  • Training and job aids. Wrote training materials, job aids, and process documentation so analysts and program staff could pick up new tracking, issue-management, and reporting workflows.
  • Executive dashboards. Built and maintained Power BI dashboards, including the Deployment Support Operations (DSO) and Event dashboards, using Power Query and DAX. I started from the question leadership needed answered and worked backward to the data.
  • Root cause analysis. Looked across data sources for patterns in recurring IT issues, documented the fixes in the knowledge base, and put preventive steps in place.
  • Automation. Used generative AI and Power Platform low-code tools to speed up documentation and cut manual reporting work.
  • Handoffs. Wrote handoff guides and standardized reporting workflows so incoming teams could keep things running through contract changes.

Results

  • Program staff adopted the new tracking and reporting workflows.
  • Leadership got a clear view of operational data across the program.
  • Recurring issues were documented and prevented, reducing disruptions for clinical staff and veterans.
  • Work continued without interruption across team and contract transitions.

How it applies to higher education

Colleges face the same challenges when they roll out a new student information system or LMS, prepare for accreditation, or try to see how programs are doing across departments. It comes down to training people on new processes, getting the right data to decision-makers, and making sure knowledge doesn't walk out the door when staff change.

Research

My doctoral research

Doctor of Education in Educational Leadership · University of Phoenix · 2026

Instructional Balance in Community College Health Sciences Programs A Qualitative Classical Grounded Theory Study

Health sciences students learn in two places: the classroom and clinical practice. Programs balance those differently, but nobody had explained how students and faculty actually deal with the difference between the two. That's what I set out to study.

I surveyed 18 students and faculty at community colleges in the Great Lakes region. Their shared main concern was the gap between what they learned in class and what clinical work actually required.

Method

Qualitative classical grounded theory

Data

Anonymous, open-ended online surveys

Participants

18 total: 12 students, 6 faculty

Analysis

Constant comparative method

Read the full abstract

Community college Health Sciences programs in the United States prepare learners for professional entry through a combination of theoretical and practical instruction. While variations in instructional balance have been documented, the underlying social processes stakeholders use to navigate these environments remained largely unexplained prior to this study. The purpose of this qualitative, classical grounded theory study was to discover the process through which students and faculty engaged with instructional balance in community college Health Sciences programs across the Great Lakes region. Data were collected via anonymous, open-ended electronic surveys from an analytic sample of n = 18 participants, including 12 students and 6 faculty members. Constant comparative analysis revealed that participants' main concern was the gap between classroom instruction and clinical or professional practice. The emergent core category, bridging the taught-and-lived gap, described the Basic Social Process unfolding across five stages, namely Encountering, Diagnosing, Mobilizing, Enacting, and Consolidating. The study identified that role position determines the mechanisms accessible for enactment, with students focusing on self-directed study systems and faculty implementing pedagogical adjustments. These findings implied that institutional leaders should reframe program goals to focus on managing rather than eliminating the recurring theory–practice gap.

Core finding

Bridging the taught-and-lived gap

The five stages people went through

  1. Encountering
  2. Diagnosing
  3. Mobilizing
  4. Enacting
  5. Consolidating

What I found

Students and faculty had the same concern, but they dealt with it in different ways because of their roles.

Students

Built their own self-directed study systems to make up for the distance between what was taught and what practice demanded.

Faculty

Made changes to their teaching within the limits of the program as it was designed.

Why it matters for college leaders: the gap between theory and practice isn't going away, so programs should plan to manage it rather than try to eliminate it. That means talking about it openly, supporting students and faculty in the different ways each can actually act, and keeping track of what works so the next group doesn't start from scratch.

Philosophy

How I lead and how I teach

If you'd like a deeper sense of how I think, here are my leadership and teaching philosophy statements.

Leadership philosophy

In my experience, programs rarely fail because of the curriculum or the people. They struggle in the space between what a program says it delivers and what its graduates can actually do on their first day on the job. My research focused on that space, and most of my career has been spent working in it. I don't think the goal is a program with no gap. The goal is a program that knows where its gap is and has a plan for it.

Talk about the gap openly

In my study, students and faculty both named the same main concern. Leaders who invite that conversation get useful information. Leaders who treat it as criticism get silence. I'd rather run a program that can name its weak spots than one that reports clean numbers nobody believes.

Support each role differently

My research showed that a person's role decides what they can do about a problem. Students built their own study systems; faculty adjusted their teaching. A single initiative aimed at everyone usually underperforms. Good leadership asks who can act on what, and gives each group the support that fits.

Make improvements last

I've been through enough transitions to know the real risk is what gets lost afterward. Documentation, clear processes, and honest handoffs are how an organization keeps what it learned. I build them in from the start, assuming I won't be the one running the program forever.

Use data to inform decisions, not make them

I spend a lot of time making data easy to understand through dashboards, KPIs, and root cause analysis. That work only matters when it helps someone make a better decision. Numbers are an input to judgment, not a replacement for it.

Teaching and learning philosophy

I've taught soldiers, field technicians, project managers, and executives. They all had what every adult learner has: real experience, limited time, and a practical reason for being there. Adult learning theory (andragogy) isn't just a framework I studied. It's what has worked with every group I've taught.

Build on what learners already know

Adults come in with their own understanding of the subject. I look for where that understanding is solid and build on it, and where it's off and address it directly. Ignoring someone's experience wastes their best resource.

Tie every objective to something people can do

I write learning outcomes as things a person can be seen doing, and I match assessments to them. If I can't describe what good performance looks like, the objective isn't ready yet.

Pick the format that fits the content

Blended learning isn't a compromise. It's a decision about which content needs people in a room and which doesn't. Step-by-step procedures usually work well online. Judgment calls and sensitive topics usually don't. I've designed both, and I've traveled onsite when the material called for it.

Leave behind materials people keep using

Quick-reference guides, knowledge-base articles, SOPs, and facilitator notes keep helping after the session ends. They're often the most valuable part of the training, because they help everyone who joins later.

Use assessment as feedback

Assessment should tell the learner and me what to do next. When results are disappointing, I look at the design first: the order of the material, the chances to practice, and how clear the expectations were.
